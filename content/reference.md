## Coaching Policy

The rules this plan is adjusted by, written down so an adjustment can be checked
against them rather than argued each time.

### What makes training effective

This list is first on purpose. Read a policy that opens with limits and caps, and
you end up with a plan that is safe and under-dosed — which is exactly what
happened to the previous version of this block.

In order of how much they move the finish time:

- **Weekly running volume** — the strongest modifiable predictor. Under 20mi/wk is under-dosed for a time goal; 30–40mi/wk is normal for the distance.
- **Long run length** — over 13 miles associates with faster finishes and with less pace decay in the second half.
- **Run frequency** — four to five days beats three at matched volume, and more to the point, it is what makes the volume reachable.
- **Intensity distribution** — roughly 80% easy, 20% hard, with easy genuinely easy. A large middle costs recovery without buying the adaptation of either end.
- **Race-pace specificity in the final third** — goal pace has to become the day's default rather than an ambition, which takes volume at that pace.
- **Heavy, low-rep strength** for running economy. Strength also serves a second and independent goal here (see Strength below), so upper-body hypertrophy work is not surplus to be trimmed when running volume rises.

**Do not cut volume to manage risk without a signal.** Higher volume and longer
long runs associate with faster times *and* with no increase in injury risk. Cut
when the recovery data says to, not pre-emptively.

### Progression

A long run does not exceed roughly 10% of the longest run of the previous 30
days. The weekly-volume version of the ten-percent rule has no evidence behind
it; the single-session version does.

Two limits on that rule. It is a guide to a safe *rate of increase*, not a
ceiling on what distance is achievable — reading it as the latter produces
absurdly timid plans. And re-attaining a distance the body has held within the
last several months is not the same as exceeding a lifetime maximum; below that
mark, larger steps are fine. The rule bites hardest in genuinely new territory.

**Cutback weeks.** Every third or fourth week drops to roughly 70%. Not optional:
it is where the adaptation lands. A cutback does not reset the progression anchor.

### Recovery

**Sleep is a training input, not a lifestyle detail.** In endurance athletes,
sleeping under 7 hours is associated with roughly 51% higher injury risk, and
poorer sleep quality with about 36% more running injuries in recreational
runners. That is a bigger, better-supported effect than any load ratio.

- With 3 or more of the last 7 nights under 7 hours, hold the long run at the
  previous week's distance rather than progressing. Repeat the week; do not
  rewrite the block.
- Three consecutive low readiness or HRV mornings is a signal. One is not.
  Wearable composite scores have no independent validation as absolute numbers;
  they are trend instruments.

**Non-training stress counts as load.** A sustained run above the usual baseline
(around 20 average) is not a neutral backdrop to a build week.

**Minimum viable week — a floor for bad weeks, not the plan.** When a week falls
apart, two sessions still happen: the Sunday long run and one strength session. A
week reduced to these two is a salvaged week. It is not the target, and it is not
what a normal week should look like.

### Strength

Strength here serves two goals at once, and they want different things:

- **Legs (anterior and posterior) — heavy and brief.** Low reps at high load is
  the stimulus that improves running economy, and it leaves far less soreness
  than high-rep work, which matters when the same legs owe a 12-mile run on
  Sunday.
- **Upper body — hypertrophy, unchanged.** It does not interfere with running, so
  running priorities are no reason to touch it.

Frequency per muscle group barely changes hypertrophy at matched weekly volume,
so the three-way split is a preference worth keeping rather than a compromise.

**Sequencing.** Endurance first on any shared day, or at least three hours apart.
Never heavy lower-body work within eight hours before a quality run.

### Swimming

Swimming is a technique sport, so frequency beats duration — three short swims a
week teaches more than one long one. That makes the current single session a
starting position rather than a dose: it exists to establish pool access and the
habit, and the second session gets added by swapping a bike once access has held
for three or four consecutive weeks.

**Treat pool logistics as part of the training, because it is the part that has
actually failed.** A swim was on the calendar for the whole previous block and
none happened; the record shows no swim in 90 days. When a session repeatedly
fails to occur, the fix is upstream of the schedule.

**Logging convention — log what one repetition moves.** Both limbs at once
(flyes, lateral raises, goblet squats, machines, barbells) is the total of both
weights, so 25lb in each hand is logged as 50. One limb per rep (split squats,
single-arm work) is the single weight, recorded as a separate set per side.
Machine and barbell lifts are inherently total, so per-side dumbbell entries in
the same session would put two different units into one volume sum. Keep it
fixed: switching mid-block puts a step change in the data that is not training.

Garmin's auto-detection records no exercise names, only a coarse category — a lat
pulldown logs as PULL_UP, an incline machine press as BENCH_PRESS. Per-exercise
progression is not trackable unless exercises are set explicitly in the Garmin
workout. Strength tracking here is session volume only.

### Injury prevention, in order of evidence

Strength training twice a week is first — roughly 50% injury reduction, and
dose-responsive. Adequate sleep is second. Gradual single-session progression is
third. Static stretching and mobility work show no effect on all-cause injury and
are last; they are kept for range of motion and comfort, not as protection.
Mobility never substitutes for a strength session.

## Training Zones

**Run heart rate** (LTHR 180bpm):

| Zone | Range |
|---|---|
| Easy / Z2 | 145–160bpm |
| Steady | 161–169bpm |
| Tempo | 170–178bpm |
| Threshold | 179–184bpm |
| Hard / VO2max | 185bpm+ |

Easy tops out at 160, not 169. At LTHR 180, 169bpm is 94% of threshold — that is
tempo wearing an easy label, and it is why the aerobic load balance came out
inverted. The 161–169 band is real training, it just is not easy training.

Garmin's threshold *pace* value is a bad reading (it computes to over an hour per
mile). Heart rate is the reliable number here.

**Bike power** (FTP 182W):

| Zone | % FTP | Watts |
|---|---|---|
| Z1 Recovery | under 55% | under 100W |
| Z2 Endurance | 55–75% | 100–136W |
| Z3 Tempo | 75–90% | 136–164W |
| Z4 Threshold | 90–105% | 164–191W |
| Z5 VO2max | 105–120% | 191–218W |

## Session Library

**Monday — Swim: Technique** — about 30min, easy throughout
- Prescribe by distance and effort, never by pace — there is no pace baseline yet and inventing one gives a target that is meaningless or discouraging
- 600–900m total to start, as short intervals with generous rest: 8 x 25m or 8 x 50m, resting until genuinely recovered
- Continuous swimming is the wrong tool at this stage — form falls apart and the reps then reinforce the broken version
- Do not ramp the volume for the first three weeks. Turning up is the entire goal; distance comes after the habit and the stroke exist
- If Sunday's long run was hard or the legs are heavy, this is a rest day instead

**Tuesday — Run: Quality** — progresses by phase rather than rotating on a fixed cycle
- Weeks 1–5, base: 10min warmup then 15–20min continuous at 170–178bpm then 10min cooldown
- Weeks 6–10, build: 10min warmup then 2 x 10min at 179–184bpm with 3min jog then 10min cooldown; from week 6 replace one rep with 10min at 8:30/mi
- Weeks 11–14, specific: 10min warmup then 3–4 x 1mi at 8:30/mi with 2min jog then 10min cooldown

**Tuesday — Strength: Upper Body** — about 35min, hypertrophy reps of 6–12
- Lat pulldown — 3 x 6–10
- Incline machine chest press — 3 x 6–10
- Close grip row or T-bar row — 2 x 8–12
- Cable or dumbbell fly — 2 x 8–12
- Cable lateral raises — 2 x 10–12
- Rear delt fly — 2 x 10–12
- Dumbbell curl — 2 x 8–12
- Cable triceps extensions — 2 x 8–12

**Wednesday — Bike: Easy** — indoor, 40–45min steady Z2 (100–136W), cadence 85–95rpm. Z2 only: adding bike intensity while run volume is ramping is how people break.

**Wednesday — Strength: Anterior Chain** — about 30min, heavy reps of 3–5
- Back or goblet squat — 4 x 3–5, heavy
- Kettlebell knee-ups — 2 x 12
- Tibia raises — 2 x 12
- Machine shoulder press — 2 x 6–10

**Thursday — Run: Easy** — 30–40min at 145–160bpm. Genuinely easy. Not optional: it is one of the four runs that make the weekly volume.

**Friday — Bike: Easy** — same session as Wednesday. Both bikes are here for the 70.3 and Ironman arc; they are the first thing to drop in a bad week.

**Friday — Strength: Posterior Chain** — about 30min, heavy reps of 3–5
- Romanian deadlift — 4 x 3–5, heavy
- Standing calf raise — 3 x 6–8, heavy
- Hyperextension or Bulgarian split squat — 2 x 8 per side
- Box jumps — 1 x 8

**Saturday — Run: Easy/Steady** — 40–60min, mostly 145–160bpm with the option to drift into 161–169 when it feels good. This is the run that took the old plan's bike slot and it is where most of the added weekly volume lives.

**Sunday — Long Run** — the distance in the progression table, at 145–160bpm. Run it continuously; if the heart rate will not hold in the band at that pace, slow down rather than breaking it up. From week 10 the last 2–3 miles are at 8:30/mi.
